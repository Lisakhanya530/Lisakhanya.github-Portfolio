Agile Evidence: WIL Project (LeafScan AI)

Our team, Data Experts, built LeafScan AI (also called Crop Doctor AI), a “doctor in your pocket” for farmers, and managed the work in Agile using Trello.

The problem

Expert help for diagnosing plant disease is slow and expensive for small-scale farmers. Existing AI tools are trained on clean lab photos and fail in real field conditions, and most only give a disease name, not a cure.

Our solution

A mobile-first app where a farmer photographs a leaf. Two AI models (ResNet50 and DenseNet121) check the diagnosis, and Google Gemini explains the symptoms, treatment and prevention in plain language.

01Agile Team Charter

Team purpose, members, roles, values and working agreements.

Evidence

Team: Data Experts · Project: LeafScan AI · Framework: Kanban · Tool: Trello

1. Team purpose and vision

Mission: the mission of Data Experts is to develop LeafScan AI, an AI-powered crop disease detection application that helps farmers identify potential plant diseases and access understandable guidance on treatment and prevention. Our vision is a practical digital assistant, a “doctor in your pocket”, that supports informed decisions about crop health.

Product: LeafScan AI is a mobile-first application that analyses photographs of plant leaves. It combines two computer vision models, ResNet50 and DenseNet121, for disease classification, and uses Google Gemini for additional guidance on symptoms, treatment and prevention. The interface is intended for mobile and desktop use.

Definition of success: a clear, usable journey from image upload to diagnostic results and recommended actions, judged on usability, functional performance, actionable guidance, responsive design and reliability (errors and unavailable AI services handled clearly).

A response time of under five seconds is a proposed performance target. It has not been confirmed as an agreed requirement or a verified test result.

02User Stories and Tasks

User needs, acceptance criteria, task breakdown and responsibilities.

Evidence

User stories for LeafScan AI, written from the user’s point of view. Each story has acceptance criteria and a task breakdown with the team member responsible.

Drafted from the LeafScan AI project documentation. Task owners are assigned by team role (Lisakhanya: UX/UI Designer, Zarha: Researcher, Lubabalo: Backend Developer, Sesona: Project Manager, Tebogo: Database) and should be checked against the team’s Trello board.

US1 · Upload or take a photo

As a farmer or gardener, I want to take or upload a photo of a leaf so that I can get a diagnosis without waiting for an expert.

Acceptance criteria

  • I can choose the camera or an image from my gallery
  • I see a preview of the image before it is analysed
  • An unsupported or failed upload shows a clear error message

Tasks and owners

  • Lisakhanya: Design the Analyse Plant screen and upload flow
  • Lisakhanya: Design error and loading states
  • Lubabalo: Build the image-upload endpoint in FastAPI
  • Sesona: Track the story on the Trello board
US2 · See the diagnosis and confidence

As a farmer, I want to see the plant, the disease and a confidence percentage so that I can judge how reliable the result is.

Acceptance criteria

  • The result shows the plant and disease name
  • A confidence percentage is shown
  • The result is labelled clearly (for example “Likely Disease”)

Tasks and owners

  • Lubabalo: Combine ResNet50 and DenseNet121 predictions (ensemble)
  • Lisakhanya: Design the result card, confidence badge and colour states
  • Zarha: Research disease classes and how to word confidence for users
US3 · Understand symptoms and what to do

As a farmer, I want plain-language symptoms, recommended actions and prevention advice so that I know how to treat and protect my crop.

Acceptance criteria

  • A Symptoms & Overview section is shown
  • Recommended Actions are listed as a clear checklist
  • Prevention guidance is included

Tasks and owners

  • Lubabalo: Integrate the Gemini API to generate guidance
  • Zarha: Check the advice for accuracy and plain language
  • Lisakhanya: Design the Symptoms & Overview and Recommended Actions layout
US4 · Ask follow-up questions

As a farmer, I want to ask questions about the disease so that I can get extra help without searching elsewhere.

Acceptance criteria

  • A chat is available from the result
  • Answers relate to the diagnosed disease

Tasks and owners

  • Lubabalo: Build the chatbot endpoint using Gemini
  • Lisakhanya: Design the floating chatbot component
US5 · Export a PDF report

As a farmer, I want to save the diagnosis and treatment plan as a PDF so that I can keep or share it.

Acceptance criteria

  • A PDF can be downloaded from the result
  • The PDF contains the diagnosis and treatment plan

Tasks and owners

  • Lisakhanya: Design the report layout
  • Lubabalo: Implement PDF export
US6 · See my dashboard

As a regular user, I want a dashboard with total scans, diseases found, healthy plants and recent analyses so that I can monitor my crops at a glance.

Acceptance criteria

  • The dashboard shows total scans, diseases found and healthy plants
  • Recent analyses are listed

Tasks and owners

  • Lisakhanya: Design the dashboard cards and Recent Analyses list
  • Tebogo: Store and organise scan results for the dashboard
  • Lubabalo: Provide the data to the frontend through the API
US7 · Review my scan history

As a user, I want to see my past scans so that I can compare results over time.

Acceptance criteria

  • A History page lists previous analyses
  • I can open a past result

Tasks and owners

  • Lisakhanya: Design the History page
  • Tebogo: Store scan history
  • Lubabalo: Expose history through the API
US8 · Use it on my phone and on a computer

As a user, I want the app to work well on a phone and a desktop so that I can use it in the field or at home.

Acceptance criteria

  • The app can be installed on iOS and Android as a PWA
  • Mobile uses a bottom navigation (Dashboard, Analyse Plant, History)
  • Desktop uses a sidebar

Tasks and owners

  • Lisakhanya: Design the responsive layouts and navigation
  • Lisakhanya: Run usability checks on mobile and desktop
  • Sesona: Coordinate testing across devices
US9 · Get reliable results on real field photos

As a farmer, I want the app to work on messy, real-world photos so that I can trust it in my field, not just on lab images.

Acceptance criteria

  • The models are trained on lab and field images
  • Results are checked on real-world photos

Tasks and owners

  • Zarha: Research the PlantVillage and PlantDoc datasets
  • Tebogo: Organise and merge the two datasets
  • Lubabalo: Train, test and integrate the models

  • US1: Design the Analyse Plant screen and upload flow
  • US1: Design error and loading states
  • US2: Design the result card, confidence badge and colour states
  • US3: Design the Symptoms & Overview and Recommended Actions layout
  • US4: Design the floating chatbot component
  • US5: Design the report layout
  • US6: Design the dashboard cards and Recent Analyses list
  • US7: Design the History page
  • US8: Design the responsive layouts and navigation

Reflection

As the UX/UI Designer, writing the user stories helped me look at LeafScan AI through the eyes of a farmer or gardener instead of through the technology. Stories such as uploading a leaf photo, understanding the diagnosis and knowing what to do next gave me a clear reason for every screen I designed, and the acceptance criteria told me what each screen had to show, for example the confidence percentage, a clear error message and a simple checklist of recommended actions.

Breaking the stories into tasks made responsibilities clear. My tasks covered the upload flow, the result card, the symptoms and recommended actions layout, the dashboard, the history page, the responsive navigation and usability checks, and I could see where my work depended on the backend and AI tasks. The hardest part was presenting technical AI results in a way a non-technical user can understand, while keeping the experience consistent on a phone and on a desktop.

I learned that user stories keep a team focused on the people they are building for, and that clear tasks and owners reduce confusion. Next time, I would check my designs against the acceptance criteria more often, and test them with real farmers earlier so that their feedback can shape the stories.

Reflection

As the UX/UI Designer, I used the product backlog to understand where my work fitted into the whole project. Designing the frontend interface is the second item, straight after planning and requirements, which meant the screens needed to be ready early so the backend and AI work had something to connect to.

The order of the backlog also taught me how the pieces depend on each other. The interface had to be designed around what the AI models and Gemini would return, such as the diagnosis, the confidence percentage, the symptoms and the recommended actions. Supporting features like the chat, history and PDF export sit later in the list, so I focused first on the core journey from uploading a leaf photo to understanding the result.

I learned that a prioritised backlog helps a team decide what matters most and keeps design decisions linked to real user needs. Next time, I would review the backlog with the team more often so that design changes and priorities are updated together.

Reflection

Seeing the whole product backlog turn into Sprint 1 showed me how quickly a project moves when the work is broken into small, checkable tasks. As the UX/UI Designer, my card was Design Frontend Interface, with a checklist to create the application layout, design the navigation and home screen, create the image upload section and design the diagnosis results section. It was due on 16 September, before the backend and AI cards that depended on it, so the designs had to be clear and complete early in the sprint.

The checklists made progress easy to see. Each card showed how many items were done, and watching every bar reach 100% helped the team stay organised and know what was left. The sprint was short, with due dates between 15 and 18 September, so I learned to prioritise the core screens first and keep my decisions simple.

Next time, I would add time for usability testing and design adjustments after the screens are connected to the backend, because the final card, Test and Present the Application, came right at the end of the sprint. I would also keep the team updated on small design changes so that the frontend and backend stay aligned.
